# AI-Slop Checklist

Organised by failure mode, not by grammar category. Each section names the thing the reader
experiences, then lists what causes it. That ordering matters when you write findings: a reader
never thinks "that's a participle phrase," they think "this is dodging the point."

Seven modes. Work through them in order — the early ones account for most real findings.

---

## 1. Throat-clearing

The copy announces that it is about to say something instead of saying it. Every one of these
can be deleted whole, and the sentence after it becomes the opening line.

**Warm-up openers**
- EN: "Here's the thing", "Here's what/this/that", "The uncomfortable truth is", "It turns out", "The real [X] is", "Let me be clear", "The truth is,", "I'll say it again:", "I'm going to be honest", "Can we talk about"
- NL: "Het punt is dit:", "Waar het om gaat is", "De ongemakkelijke waarheid is", "Het blijkt dat", "De echte [X] is", "Laat me duidelijk zijn", "Ik zeg het nog een keer:", "Kunnen we het even hebben over"

**Stage directions.** Copy narrating its own shape: "Hint:", "Plot twist:", "You already know this, but", "Let me walk you through", "In this section we'll", "Let's dive in", "Here's what you need to know". Both languages, identical pattern. Start instead.

**Rhetorical wind-ups.** "What if [reframe]?", "Here's what I mean:", "Think about it:". These promise an insight and then make the reader wait for it. Deliver the insight.

**Scene-setters.** "In today's [X]", "In a world where", "At its core", "At the end of the day", "The reality is", "It's worth noting" / NL: "In de kern", "Het is het vermelden waard", "In een wereld waarin".

---

## 2. Padding

Words that occupy space without changing meaning. Test: delete the word and read the sentence
again. If nothing was lost, it was padding.

**Adverbs.** Cut nearly all: really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, deeply,
truly, fundamentally, interestingly, importantly, crucially / NL: echt, gewoon, letterlijk, oprecht,
eerlijk gezegd, simpelweg, eigenlijk, nogmaals.

**Emphasis crutches.** Instructions to the reader about how strongly to feel: "Full stop.", "Let that
sink in.", "Make no mistake", "This matters because" / NL: "Punt. Uit.", "Laat dat even bezinken.",
"Vergis je niet". Strong copy earns emphasis; it does not request it.

**Lazy extremes.** "every", "always", "never", "everyone", "no one". Almost always an unearned
absolute standing in for a specific number or example.

---

## 3. Borrowed vocabulary

Words the model reaches for because they appear everywhere in its training, not because they fit
this sentence. They read as competent and say very little.

**Model-favourite words:** delve, crucial, intricate, pivotal, tapestry, testament, underscore,
garner, foster, enhance, align with, landscape (used as an abstract noun), robust, seamless, leverage.

**Business jargon:**

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| Navigate (challenges) | Handle, solve |
| Unpack (analysis) | Explain, examine |
| Landscape (context) | Situation, field |
| Gamechanger | Important, significant |
| Double down on | Invest more in |
| Deep dive | Analysis, look |
| Stakeholders | The people involved |
| Impactful | Effective |

**Aphorism formulas.** "X is the Y of Z", "X is not a tool but a mirror". The shape of wisdom with
no claim inside it. Replace with the specific thing being asserted.

---

## 4. Manufactured drama

Structures that simulate tension the content has not earned. This is the mode that separates
adequate copy from copy that sounds machine-made — and the one with the biggest exception
attached, so read section 8 before flagging anything here.

**Binary contrasts.** "Not because X. Because Y." / "The answer isn't X, it's Y." / "It feels like X.
It's actually Y." The reversal is visible from the first clause. State the conclusion.

**Negative listing.** "No X. No Y. A Z." A striptease before an ordinary point. State Z.

**Period-stacking.** Verbless fragments in sequence: "Four minutes. On the toilet. Scrolling."
Two measurable tests:
- More than 10 full stops per 100 words in spoken or UGC-style copy is a problem; more than 13 is an automatic rewrite.
- Three or more consecutive sentences under 8 words is fragmentation. Rejoin them with and, but, because, while, so.

**Rule-of-three lists.** Two items or one. Three is the rhythm a model defaults to.

**The one-liner habit.** Every paragraph landing on a short punchy closing sentence.

**Em-dash conversion damage.** After an em dash is removed, inspect the full stop left behind. It
usually wanted to be a comma, or "and" / "but" / "because" — not a sentence break.

---

## 5. Distance from the subject

Nobody is present in the sentence. No person acts, and the writer observes from somewhere above
the scene. This is the quietest tell and often the most damaging, because the copy stops feeling
like it came from anyone.

**Passive constructions.** Every sentence wants a human subject performing a verb. "Mistakes were
made" → name who made them.

**Objects doing human things.** "The complaint becomes a solution", "the decision emerges", "the
data tells us". Abstractions cannot act. Name the person who did it.

**Narration from orbit.** "Nobody designed this", "This happens because". Put the reader inside the
scene. "You" outperforms "people" nearly every time.

**Copula avoidance.** "serves as", "stands as", "represents" where "is" would do. "Gallery 825
serves as the exhibition space" → "Gallery 825 is the exhibition space."

**Participle tails.** "...highlighting the brand's commitment", "...reflecting a broader shift".
A clause appended to manufacture significance. Cut it.

**Question-word openers.** Sentences beginning What / When / Where / Which / Who / Why / How.
Restructure to lead with the subject or the verb.

---

## 6. Evasion

The copy appears to make a claim but cannot be held to one.

**Unnamed specifics.** "The reasons are structural", "The implications are significant". Name the
reason, or delete the sentence.

**Dressed-up guesses.** "not extensively documented", "it is believed that", "likely grew up in".
State plainly what is not known. Never style a guess as a finding.

**False ranges.** "from X to Y" where X and Y do not sit on any real scale.

**Synonym cycling.** The same thing renamed four ways in one piece to avoid repeating a word —
"the protagonist… the main character… the central figure". Repetition is fine. Use the same word.

**Flattery.** "Great question!", "You're absolutely right". No place in marketing copy.

---

## 7. Surface tells

Cheap to check, and they cluster with everything above.

Curly quotes in copy meant for plain text. Emoji in headings. Title Case In Headings. Bold applied
to whole sentences instead of single terms. Hyphenated pairs after the noun ("the report is
high-quality" wants "high quality").

---

## 8. The ad-copy exception — read before flagging anything in section 4

Staccato rhythm, hard contrast and triplets are deliberate, tested techniques in **ad copy**: the
primary text, caption or headline running with a static, video or UGC ad. Direct-response training
teaches them on purpose, and they work. The identical pattern inside a LinkedIn message, cold
email, organic post or organic caption reads as manufactured.

**Channel is ad copy:** flag section 4 only when a pattern is extreme — well beyond the numeric
thresholds above — or clearly accidental, such as fallout from an em-dash conversion rather than a
deliberate hook.

**Every other channel:** section 4 applies in full.

---

## 9. Em dashes are language-dependent

Read this before applying the cluster rule in section 10.

**Dutch:** a single em dash (U+2014) is a tell on its own — `Confidence: High` in isolation. Dutch
typography uses a spaced en dash (–) for the *gedachtestreepje*. An unspaced em dash is rare enough
in native Dutch commercial writing that it usually indicates text pasted straight out of a model.
Flag it with no supporting evidence.

**English:** the cluster rule holds unchanged. Editors and journalists use em dashes constantly.
One proves nothing.

---

## 10. What not to flag

A careful human writer will trip several patterns above with no AI involvement anywhere. On their
own, none of these are evidence:

- Clean grammar and polished style. Professionals write well.
- Casual and formal register mixed inside one piece.
- Formal vocabulary used correctly. Do not flatten a real word for sounding educated.
- A single dash, one "however", one adverb — **English only**; see section 9 for Dutch.
- A transition word (additionally, moreover) used once. Only a tell when stacked.
- Tidy formatting from a template or a copy editor.
- Uncited claims. Marketing copy does not carry footnotes.

**Flag clusters, not instances** — the Dutch em dash in section 9 is the single exception. One em
dash in English copy is noise. Em dashes plus a triplet plus "vibrant tapestry" plus a summarising
wrap-up, all in one piece, is signal.

---

## 11. Protect these

Signs a human wrote it. If you find them, leave them alone — editing them out is how a review makes
copy worse.

- A specific detail nobody would invent: an odd number, a stray aside, a real name.
- Unresolved tension, or mixed feelings, instead of a tidy conclusion.
- Real self-correction mid-thought.
- Sentence lengths that vary inside a single paragraph.
- A first-person opinion the writer could defend if challenged.
