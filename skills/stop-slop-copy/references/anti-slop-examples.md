# Before / After Examples

## Dutch

**Example 1: Empty opener + binary contrast**

Before:
> "Het punt is dit: producten bouwen is moeilijk. Niet omdat de technologie complex is. Maar omdat mensen complex zijn. Laat dat even bezinken."

After:
> "Producten bouwen is moeilijk. Technologie is beheersbaar. Mensen niet."

Changes: removed the opener, removed the binary-contrast structure, removed the emphasis crutch. Direct statements instead.

**Example 2: Filler words + unnecessary reassurance**

Before:
> "Het blijkt dat de meeste teams worstelen met afstemming. De ongemakkelijke waarheid is dat niemand wil toegeven dat ze in de war zijn. En dat is oké."

After:
> "Teams worstelen met afstemming. Niemand geeft verwarring toe."

Changes: removed the hedge ("de meeste"), removed the opener phrases, removed the permission-granting ending.

**Example 3: Business jargon stacking**

Before:
> "In het huidige snel veranderende landschap moeten we ongemak omarmen en door onzekerheid navigeren met helderheid. Dit is belangrijk omdat je concurrentie niet wacht."

After:
> "Beweeg sneller. Je concurrentie doet het al."

Changes: cut all the jargon. Core message in six words.

**Example 4: Dramatic fragmentation**

Before:
> "Snelheid. Kwaliteit. Kosten. Je kunt er maar twee kiezen. Dat is het. Dat is de afweging."

After:
> "Snelheid, kwaliteit, kosten — kies er twee."

Changes: one sentence, no performative emphasis. (Note: this is fine as a single punchy line — the problem in the "before" was stacking four short fragments in a row, not the concept itself.)

**Example 5: Rhetorical setup**

Before:
> "Wat als ik je zou vertellen dat de beste teams niet optimaliseren voor productiviteit? Dit is wat ik bedoel: ze optimaliseren voor leren. Denk er eens over na."

After:
> "De beste teams optimaliseren voor leren, niet voor productiviteit."

Changes: direct statement, no rhetorical scaffolding.

## English

**Example 6: Filler phrases**

Before:
> "In order to achieve this goal, it is important to note that the system has the ability to process requests in real-time."

After:
> "To achieve this, the system processes requests in real time."

**Example 7: Corporate jargon + rule of three**

Before:
> "The event features keynote sessions, panel discussions, and networking opportunities. Attendees can expect innovation, inspiration, and industry insights."

After:
> "The event includes talks and panels, plus time for informal networking between sessions."

**Example 8: Em dashes + false emphasis**

Before:
> "The new policy — announced without warning — affects thousands of workers. The changes — long overdue according to critics — will take effect immediately."

After:
> "The new policy, announced without warning, affects thousands of workers. The changes, long overdue according to critics, take effect immediately."

**Example 9: Copula avoidance + promotional language**

Before:
> "Our platform boasts a vibrant, cutting-edge interface that showcases our commitment to enhancing the user experience."

After:
> "Our platform has a clean, fast interface built around what users actually do."

## The ad-copy exception in practice

Not everything that looks like Part 2 of the checklist is bad — in ad copy (the primary text/caption/headline on a static, video, or UGC ad), this is fine and intentional:

> "Speed. Quality. Cost. Pick two."

Same short-fragment, rule-of-two/three pattern that gets flagged as "dramatic fragmentation" in Dutch Example 4 above — but here it's a deliberate ad hook, not four stacked fragments padding out a paragraph. Don't flag this in ad copy. Flag it in a LinkedIn message, cold email, organic post, or organic caption.
